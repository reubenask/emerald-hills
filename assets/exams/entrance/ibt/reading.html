The Psychology of Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Decision-making is rarely a straightforward process. When people are faced with choices under conditions of uncertainty, they frequently rely on mental shortcuts, known as heuristics, rather than carrying out systematic analysis. While heuristics often lead to reasonably good decisions in everyday situations, they can also produce predictable errors in judgement, which psychologists refer to as cognitive biases.

One of the most studied cognitive biases is the availability heuristic, a concept developed by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s. This heuristic describes the tendency for people to judge the likelihood of an event based on how easily an example comes to mind. For instance, a person who has recently read about a plane crash may overestimate the danger of air travel, even though statistically it remains one of the safest forms of long-distance transport. The vividness and emotional impact of a recent memory makes certain risks feel more probable than they actually are.

A related phenomenon is the anchoring effect, in which the first piece of information a person encounters on a subject disproportionately influences their subsequent judgements. In one well-known experiment, participants were asked to estimate the percentage of African nations in the United Nations after being shown a randomly generated number. Those shown a higher number consistently gave higher estimates, demonstrating that even irrelevant information can serve as an anchor that skews reasoning.

Understanding these biases has significant practical implications. In fields such as medicine, law, and finance, poor decisions driven by cognitive biases can have serious consequences. As a result, many organisations now invest in training programmes designed to help professionals recognise and counteract their own biased thinking. Structured decision-making frameworks, peer review processes, and deliberate consideration of alternative viewpoints are among the strategies used to reduce the influence of heuristics on high-stakes choices.
